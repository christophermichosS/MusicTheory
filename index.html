What is music theory for?

How can we choose notes and chords on purpose, create parts that fit together, and eventually make a complete song?

Music theory is a language for describing patterns in music. It helps you understand, repeat, communicate, and deliberately change what you hear.

Guitar example

“Move that riff one octave higher,” “play the root note,” and “use the same chord progression” are theory-informed directions.

EDM / MIDI example

“Keep the melody in the same key as the chords” and “remove the kick in the breakdown” are theory and arrangement choices.

Our learning rule
  1. Hear a small example.
  2. See the pattern.
  3. Copy it.
  4. Learn its name and reason.
  5. Change one safe element.
Foundation

Understand the materials

1. Sound and notes

Sound comes from vibration. Slower vibration is heard as lower pitch; faster vibration is heard as higher pitch. A note is a named pitch.

A · B · C · D · E · F · G → repeat
C · C♯/D♭ · D · D♯/E♭ · E · F · F♯/G♭ · G · G♯/A♭ · A · A♯/B♭ · B

Guitar example

Moving one fret higher moves one small pitch step. In standard tuning, thicker strings generally start lower; thinner strings start higher.

EDM / MIDI example

In a Piano Roll, higher blocks mean higher pitch. Moving blocks right changes timing, not pitch.

Why focus on 12 notes?

This tuning system is commonly used on guitars, basses, pianos, MIDI controllers, and DAWs. Other musical traditions use different tuning systems, pitch collections, and pitches between standard piano keys. These instruments and software may also support alternative tunings.

A sharp raises a natural note one semitone; a flat lowers it one semitone. On a standard equal-tempered keyboard, C♯ and D♭ share one key. E–F and B–C are already one semitone apart: there is no additional standard keyboard key between them.

2. Octaves and range

After 12 pitch steps, the note names repeat. The next C is higher but retains the name C. The distance is called an octave.

Low C → next higher C = one octave

Guitar example

The low open E string and high open E string are both E, two octaves apart in standard tuning. Moving a riff 12 frets higher raises it one octave.

EDM / MIDI example

A low C sub-bass, middle-range C bass synth, and high C lead can reinforce the same musical idea.

Octave doubling versus harmony

Octave doubling: same note name in different ranges.

Bass: C + Guitar: higher C

Harmony: how simultaneous pitches relate. Octaves also have a harmonic relationship, but octave doubling usually reinforces one line rather than adding an independent harmony part.

C + E + G = C major chord

3. Rhythm and time

Notes tell you what pitch to play. Rhythm tells you when to play it. A beat is the steady pulse; tempo is its speed; a bar is a repeating group of beats.

4/4 time: 1 2 3 4 | 1 2 3 4

Guitar example

Strum a C chord on every beat, then on beats 1 and 3 only. Same chord, different feel.

EDM / MIDI example

Put a kick on every beat and a clap on beats 2 and 4 to create a simple dance framework.

What does 4/4 mean?

There are four quarter-note beats in each bar. Tempo is measured in beats per minute (BPM). At 100 BPM, one four-beat bar lasts 2.4 seconds.

4. Keys and scales

A key gives music a home note and a major or minor foundation. C major is useful as a first example because its scale uses the white keys on a piano/MIDI keyboard.

C major scale: C D E F G A B

C is the home note. The scale lists the available starting notes in order, but you can use them in any order when making riffs or melodies.

What do major and minor mean?

In “C major,” C tells us where home is. Major tells us the pattern of distances between the notes around that home. Minor uses a different pattern. These words do not mean loud versus quiet, or important versus unimportant.

Think of the 12-note map as all the available steps. A scale chooses certain steps. Different choices of spacing create different relationships to the home note.

First, understand the step sizes

A half step (semitone) is one fret on guitar or one adjacent piano key—including black keys. A whole step is two half steps: two guitar frets or two adjacent-key moves.

Major: whole · whole · half · whole · whole · whole · half
Natural minor: whole · half · whole · whole · half · whole · whole

You do not have to memorize these patterns today. They explain why major and minor are different—not just names we attach to a mood.

Compare the same home note

C major: C D E F G A B C
C natural minor: C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭ C

Both begin and end on C. In natural minor, E, A, and B are lowered one half step. The home stays the same, but the surrounding note relationships change.

Major often suggests a brighter or more open character; minor often suggests a darker or more reflective character. These are tendencies, not rules: tempo, rhythm, lyrics, sound choice, and context all shape the feeling.

Guitar example

Compare the notes C–E–G with C–E♭–G. Keep C and G fixed and lower E by one fret on the same string. That one change turns a C-major chord into C minor. Step 5 explains how these three-note chords are built.

EDM example

Keep a synth sound, rhythm, and low C bass note unchanged. Try C–E–G, then C–E♭–G. Changing the notes—not the synth preset—is what changes the chord from major to minor.

Same ingredients, different home

C major: C D E F G A B → home is C
A natural minor: A B C D E F G → home is A

These scales share the same seven notes, but organize them around different home notes. They are called relative major and minor. Merely starting on A does not establish A minor: the melody, chords, bass, and phrase endings help make A feel like home.

For this project: stay with C major. This comparison explains what “major” means; it is not an instruction to switch keys.

One more distinction: a major key contains both major and minor chords. A minor chord such as Am can belong to C major without changing the whole song’s key. Step 5 shows why.

We are using natural minor as our first minor-scale example. Minor-key music may also alter notes for melodic or harmonic reasons; those options come later.

Safe palette, not a prison

Use C-major notes at first so chords, basslines, and melodies can share the same material. This is a starting point, not a guarantee that every note combination will feel settled. Other notes can create deliberate colour, tension, or surprise.

5. Build chords

A chord combines notes. A triad is a three-note chord built from a root, third, and fifth. To build triads from our scale: start on a scale note, skip one, take one, skip one, then take one.

C D E F G → take C, E, G
C E G = C major
RootNotesChord
CC–E–GC major
DD–F–AD minor
EE–G–BE minor
FF–A–CF major
GG–B–DG major
AA–C–EA minor
BB–D–FB diminished

Guitar example

A C major guitar shape may repeat notes in octaves, but its core notes are C, E, and G. Power chords omit the third, so they are not clearly major or minor.

EDM / MIDI example

Draw C, E, and G vertically in a Piano Roll and play them using a pad, piano, pluck, or synth.

The number labels show which scale note a chord is built on: I = C, ii = Dm, iii = Em, IV = F, V = G, vi = Am, vii° = B diminished. Uppercase indicates major, lowercase indicates minor, and ° indicates diminished. You do not need to memorize them yet.

6. Make chord loops

A chord progression is the order in which chords appear. Building a chord and deciding what chord comes next are separate choices.

C → F → G → C

A useful first description is home, move away, tension, return home. It is a common tendency, not a compulsory rule.

How do we choose the next chord?

Guide 1: Listen for shared notes. C major and A minor both use C and E, so C → Am can feel smooth.

Guide 2: Test direction. Some chords feel stable, others create motion or expectation. Your ears choose the effect you want.

Try: C → Am | C → F | C → G

Use the same player to compare loops

Keep C major selected. Click each chord once and count four steady beats before clicking the next. Return to the first chord and repeat.

Loop A: C → Am → F → G
Loop B: C → Am → F → C

Only the final chord changes. Which version feels more settled? Which makes you want to hear the loop again? You are comparing choices, not finding one correct answer.

Add bass, a hook, and song structure

7. Add bass and hooks

Start bass by playing the root of each chord. Over C–G–Am–F, play C–G–A–F. Then create a short hook using C-major notes.

Chord loop: C → G → Am → F
Bass roots: C → G → A → F

Guitar example

Let guitar play chords while bass plays roots in time. Try copying a root one octave higher for movement.

EDM / MIDI example

Put roots in a sub-bass track, then write a two- to four-note synth hook over the chords.

Simple hook recipe
  1. Choose two to four C-major notes.
  2. Give them a rhythm.
  3. Repeat the idea.
  4. Change only the final note, rhythm, or octave.
Example: E → G → E → D

Shared starter-track template

Time: 4/4 · Tempo: 100 BPM · Key: C major

Chords: C → G → Am → F
Bass: C → G → A → F

Hook: a short pattern using C, D, E, F, G, A, and B.

8. Arrange a song

Form is the order of sections. Arrangement is deciding which layers are active in each section. A loop becomes a song when layers enter, leave, repeat, or change.

Guitar example

Intro → Verse → Chorus → Verse → Chorus → Outro. Make choruses bigger with fuller guitar, bass, drums, and a stronger hook.

EDM / MIDI example

Intro → Build → Drop → Breakdown → Build → Drop → Outro. Build expectation, bring in drums/bass/hook for the drop, then reduce energy before the return.
